# Achmad Farizky

**Data Specialist & Data Storyteller · CDMP Master · 21 years turning telecom, retail and healthcare data into decisions.**
Based between Dhahran and Madinah, Saudi Arabia.

Hi, I'm Achmad. I'm a telecommunication engineer who fell in love with data. For 21 years, I've turned messy data into reports, dashboards and stories that help people make decisions: first in telecom in Indonesia and Saudi Arabia, then in retail, and now in healthcare at Johns Hopkins Aramco Healthcare.

I learned most of what I know by doing it myself, from great mentors, and from online communities. I'm over 40 and I'm still a student, which I think is the best part.

This website is my home for stories. On LinkedIn I share the short version. Here you'll find the full one: what happened, what I learned, and the people who helped me along the way. If your background is like mine, an engineer from another field, a mid-career learner, or someone starting over, I hope something here helps you.

---

## Three threads in my story

When I look back at 21 years of work, the same three lessons keep showing up. I'll point to them along the way, so watch for the 🧵.

1. **🧵 Learn what you love, even if it's outside your job.** I studied telecom engineering, but I also taught myself design and programming. Many times, that "extra" skill is what made me useful.
2. **🧵 Stay close to people who see the business.** Most of my best work started as someone else's idea: a friend, a team leader, a manager. They saw what was needed. I figured out how to build it.
3. **🧵 Build it cheap, with what you already have.** I rarely had a big budget. Most of my solutions used the tools we already owned, with a small customization to make them do what we needed.

## My story

### 1. University: an engineer who loved design (2000–2005)

**Context**
In 2000, I started studying Telecommunication Engineering at Telkom University in Bandung, Indonesia. *(Back then, it was called Sekolah Tinggi Teknologi Telkom, or STT Telkom.)*

My classes were real engineering stuff: switching techniques, traffic engineering, transmission lines, antenna and propagation, digital signal processing, and optical and satellite communication systems. There was a lot of calculus, physics and lab work, and I enjoyed it. Programming was only a small part: one introduction to computers and one programming lab.

**What I did**
But outside the classroom, I spent a lot of my time on something else: design. I taught myself Adobe Photoshop and CorelDraw, then web design with HTML, CSS and JavaScript, and animation with Macromedia Flash. Nobody asked me to. I just liked making things look good.

I had a friend, Jockie Heruseon, who was much better than me at networking. He always knew where the opportunities were. So we teamed up: he found them, and I did the design work. We joined web design competitions, and we got projects to design the university's yearly academic book and the university calendar. Together, we learned how the real world works, long before we graduated.

I also had a best "art friend" on campus, Fakhrudin. He was a famous animator and designer among the students, and we pushed each other to make better work. Years later, when I made animated SVG visuals in Power BI, it took me straight back to those Flash days.

**What I learned**
- **🧵 Thread 1, for the first time.** Design wasn't part of my engineering degree, but I learned it anyway because I loved it. I didn't know then that it would become a big part of how I present data for the next 20 years.
- **🧵 Thread 2, also for the first time.** My friend saw the opportunities; I knew how to deliver. Neither of us could have done those projects alone. I've been working this way ever since.
- Working for a real "client" (my own university) as a student taught me that deadlines are real, and that people judge your work by what they see first.

**A moment I'm proud of**
Getting paid, as a student, to design my own university's calendar and its academic book. Those were my first real design clients.

**Tools I picked up**
Adobe Photoshop · CorelDraw · HTML · CSS · JavaScript · Macromedia Flash

### 2. Telkomsel: the desk guy who discovered data (2005–2009)

**Context**
After graduating in 2005, I joined Telkomsel, Indonesia's biggest mobile operator, as a radio network engineer in operations and network performance, first in Medan and later in Jakarta.

Honestly, I was a bit jealous in my first years. My colleagues travelled for technical training and went to the field with drive test tools. I stayed at my desk with spreadsheets. One night I begged to join them, and they sent me to a rural site at 2 AM just to make a test call. I was so happy, like a kid in an amusement park.

**What I did**
At my desk, I started doing more than delivering numbers. I learned UNIX scripting by myself and used it to build small automations for our daily work. I used my old web skills to build a web dashboard for network performance. Then I combined Google Earth with UNIX scripts to build a live GIS view of our 2G radio network, so management could see how the network was running from their own workstations.

In my last year, my manager, Akhmad Madces, brought me into a big data warehouse project. We collected network performance data from all our equipment vendors (Ericsson, Siemens, Nokia, Huawei and more) into one place, and built the dashboards and reports on top of it with Pentaho. I had two great mentors on that project: Reza Lesmana, a solution architect at Nokia, and Feris Thia, CEO of Pentaho Indonesia. It was my first time learning what a data warehouse is, and I still use those concepts today.

**What I learned**
- **🧵 Thread 1 again.** Very few people around me knew even a little programming. That small skill made me useful in a way I didn't expect.
- **🧵 Thread 2 again.** My team leaders, Petrus Sila and Sorpa EA Togatorop, kept me at my desk with Excel. At the time I didn't understand why. Later I realized they saw where my work created real value before I did. I wasn't just their "calculator guy." I was helping guide their decisions.
- **🧵 Thread 3, for the first time.** Google Earth and a few scripts became a live monitoring tool. No big budget, just tools we already had.

**A moment I'm proud of**
In 2009, after the earthquake in West Sumatra, my GIS live monitoring tool was used during the network recovery. I received official appreciation from the company's directors and VPs. I was also named best employee of the service quality assurance team in 2007.

**Tools I picked up**
UNIX shell scripting · Excel · Ericsson OSS · Google Earth (KML) · HTML/JavaScript · Pentaho (Kettle) · Data warehousing

### 3. A year of trying (2009–2010)

**Context**
At the end of 2009, I left Telkomsel to build a career overseas. It took longer than I hoped. For most of 2010, I kept applying and waiting.

**What I did**
I used that time to fill a gap. After five years in data and reporting, I felt my telecom knowledge was not deep enough. So I studied 3G radio networks seriously. I wanted to understand how things really work on the business side of the network, not only the data and the reports about it.

**What I learned**
- **🧵 Thread 2, in a different way.** To be useful to engineers, I had to understand their world, not just their numbers.
- Patience. Some doors open late, but they do open. In October 2010, Ericsson brought me to Riyadh, Saudi Arabia.

When I arrived, my English speaking was close to zero. My Arabic helped me survive, and my English just "grew by nature" for many years. (It took me another 15 years to finally fix it properly. That's its own story.)

### 4. Ericsson: 13 years with Saudi telecom operators (2010–2023)

**Context**
At Ericsson, I was a Data Solution Professional in managed services. I worked from Riyadh and Jeddah for telecom operators in Saudi Arabia, and later supported projects in the GCC and Africa. I was on the business side, close to the network operations teams, not in IT.

#### 4a. STC: building on SAP BusinessObjects (2010–2012)

My first customer was STC. Here I went deep into SAP BusinessObjects, not just as a user. I built ways to share documents publicly, set up alerts for network anomalies, and put data on Google Earth and Google Maps for faster insights.

In 2011, I was named best employee of the STC network performance team. In 2012, Omar Elward taught me Excel macros and VBA, another tool I used for years.

#### 4b. Mobily: nine years and the Hajj dream team (2013–2022)

Mobily was my longest customer. From 2013 to 2020, I was the data leader for Hajj affairs. During Hajj, the network carries millions of pilgrims in a few days, and the operations team needs to see what is happening live.

I integrated the data end to end, built dashboards and a mini-GIS application for live monitoring, made advanced reports, and automated many processes. This saved a lot of effort and improved accuracy. The tools were used for many years in a row.

That team was more than a team. It was a family: we supported each other, taught each other, and covered each other. Naif Al Malki, then a General Manager at Mobily and my data analytics mentor for many years, wrote this about my work:

> "Indeed, your fingerprint is still till today, you are one of the best talented telecom engineer in advanced data analysis field that I worked with him, wish you all the best."
> — Naif Al Malki, now GM of Service Management Center

I also received official appreciation from the customer's top management for the work progress and deep analytics.

At Mobily, I also "cheated" once. Our BI tool made it hard to compare time periods, like today vs. yesterday, hour by hour. So I added "time offset" calculations directly into our main data model on the production server. I was afraid it looked like a quick fix. But it worked for years, and it's still in use. More than ten years later, I saw well-known BI experts teaching the same idea in Power BI, and calling it a superpower.

My "cheat" had been an expert trick all along. I just didn't know it.

#### 4c. Many customers, one way of working (2021–2023)

In my last years, I moved between projects: a 4G and 5G trial for Zain, remote support for MTN Benin in Africa, then back to STC, and finally Tawal. Because I had built my tools in a reusable way, I could copy them to new projects quickly. A project manager in Bahrain told me, "Your reports are great!" A customer director in Benin said, "I like that dashboard!"

During these years I also kept learning by myself: advanced Excel and Power Query, Python, and machine learning. A friend who was also my customer, Sultan Alsayed, showed me his work in Power BI, and that pushed me to want to be as good as him. My boss also encouraged me to go deeper into machine learning.

**What I learned**
- **🧵 Thread 2 again.** Most of my best tools started from what my team leaders, managers and customers needed. The IT team was supportive too: "Go ahead, do what the business needs."
- **🧵 Thread 3 again.** The vendor's built-in reports didn't always fit our needs, so I built custom ones with the tools we had. Custom was the solution, and many of those tools are still in daily use.
- Sometimes what looks like a "cheat" is actually a smart solution. That gave me the confidence to start sharing even my small ideas.

**A moment I'm proud of**
Being trusted as the data leader for Hajj for eight seasons in a row, and Naif's words above.

**Tools I picked up**
SAP BusinessObjects (universe design, Web Intelligence) · Pentaho Data Integration (Kettle) · SQL · Excel VBA · Power Query · Python · Google Earth/Maps · ReactJS (side experiment)

### 5. Kanzway: my first manager role (2023–2025)

**Context**
In September 2023, I joined Kanzway in Jeddah in a senior data management role, to manage the company's digital product catalog. Later my role changed: I became Business and Data Management Manager, leading a small data team. It was a new world for me, far from telecom: industrial tool brands, product catalogs and business requirements.

**What I did**
I wrote business requirements documents (BRDs) to define project scope, and designed entity relationship diagrams (ERDs) for the product catalog. I built a process with Python and SQL to collect complex catalog data from our partners' websites (brands like Dormer, Völkel, ESAB, Knipex, Koken and Röhm) and load it into our database with full accuracy. I also built Power BI dashboards for management.

I also worked closely with software developers for the first time. My friends Yussin Hidayat and Aslah Fuadin taught me Git on a real project, and I learned to manage work with Trello.

In 2025 the company ended its operations. I stayed until the last months to help.

**What I learned**
- Leading a team for the first time taught me that my job was no longer only to build things, but to help other people build them well.
- Writing BRDs taught me to speak the business language first, before touching any data.
- Working with developers taught me version control and teamwork habits that I had never needed as a "solo data warrior."
- When things end, you still have everything you learned.

**Tools I picked up**
Python (web data collection) · Advanced SQL · ERD design · BRDs · Power BI · Git · Trello

### 6. Learning through community (2025)

**Context**
At the end of 2024, I decided to learn Power BI properly. I already knew BI from SAP BusinessObjects, but I wanted to learn the modern way. So I joined online communities and followed their instructions exactly.

**What I did**
- **Power BI Park** (led by Injae Park), from January 2025. One project after another, posting my progress on LinkedIn twice a week because the community asked us to. I was #1 on the monthly leaderboard from January to March, and then I was asked to be a moderator.
- **Fabric Dojo** (led by Will Needham), April 2025. I finished all the classes in one month, reached #1 on the leaderboard, and earned the blue belt.
- **The Data Vis Forge** (led by Gustaw Dudek), May–June 2025. Advanced "workouts" where I chose not to open the solution files. I learned corporate-style reporting there.
- In July, I built a simple portfolio website and started looking for a job. In August, two big companies interviewed me on the same day, and both offered me a job. My LinkedIn posts really helped.
- During the hiring process, I passed **DP-700** (Microsoft Fabric) and **PL-300** (Power BI) to make sure my foundation was solid.

**What I learned**
- **Just do the thing.** Follow the mentor's instructions exactly. Don't skip steps. I even typed the datasets myself instead of downloading them, and rebuilt the visuals instead of copying them. That's where I found the "hidden treasures."
- **Find your community.** My first community was Pentaho-Indonesia in 2010. Fifteen years later, communities changed my career again.
- **🧵 Thread 1 again.** I was over 40, with 20 years of experience, and still a student. Age is just a number.

**A moment I'm proud of**
Two job offers on the same day, after only eight months of learning in public.

**Tools I picked up**
Power BI (DAX, TMDL, field parameters, visual calculations, SVG) · Tabular Editor (C# macros) · JSON themes · Microsoft Fabric (lakehouse, warehouse, pipelines, real-time, CI/CD) · Azure DevOps · Figma (icons)

### 7. Johns Hopkins Aramco Healthcare: learning a new language (2025–now)

**Context**
In October 2025, I joined Johns Hopkins Aramco Healthcare (JHAH) in Dhahran as a Quality & Data Analyst, working as a data specialist in healthcare. It was my third industry, and the first one where I didn't know the business at all.

**What I did**
Before day one, I studied healthcare analytics and passed the **CHDA** exam (AHIMA), which shocked me with how deep the healthcare questions were. Then Elijah Joshua Pryor, through his posts, introduced me to DAMA and the DMBOK2. After that, I started studying data management seriously, because it brings all my years of experience together into one framework.

In my first year:
- I found that about 60% of my job is talking to people, not working with data.
- My first task was one page of a Power BI dashboard. A few weeks later, I was unexpectedly asked to open it in a meeting with shareholders and answer their questions. They liked it.
- My director gave me the chance to lead a new data project, where I practiced data modeling for real.
- I took Epic training (Cogito, Clarity, Caboodle and Access data models) to understand our healthcare data and work better with the IT reporting teams.
- I helped with the data side of JHAH's ICHOM accreditation for two services, one of them a first in Saudi Arabia.
- I passed all my CDMP exams and became a **CDMP Master** in August 2026.

My work moved from front-end dashboards to the back end: data modeling, data quality and metadata. I miss building Power BI experiments sometimes, but a strong back end makes the front end much easier.

Now I'm studying again: a **Master of Applied Science in Patient Safety & Healthcare Quality** at the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health, and two **AI and Agentic AI in Healthcare** programs at the Johns Hopkins Whiting School of Engineering.

**What I learned**
- **🧵 Thread 2, in its newest form: learn their language.** To build something useful for doctors, nurses and pharmacists, I first need to understand how they work. So I'm learning healthcare from the inside.
- **Find mentors early.** My colleagues are senior people with clinical backgrounds, and my manager is strong in data management. I'm surrounded by great teachers.
- **Analytics shows you the answer. Data management tells you whether you can trust it.**

**A moment I'm proud of**
That shareholder meeting, when the dashboard answered their questions better than I could.

**Tools I picked up**
DAMA-DMBOK (data modeling, DW/BI, data quality) · Epic Clarity/Caboodle/Cogito · Healthcare statistics · Power BI · PowerPoint (yes, still)

## Tips for people with a background like mine

For engineers from non-IT fields who want to move into data, for anyone mid-career who wants to learn something new, and for anyone starting over.

1. **Learn the small skill nobody around you has.** A little programming made me useful in a telecom team full of hardware engineers. You don't need to be an expert, just a bit ahead.
2. **Stay close to the business.** Step away from your desk. Ask people about their daily problems. Learn their language before you build anything.
3. **Build with what you already have.** Expensive tools don't make you valuable. Solving a real problem with existing tools, in a way your colleagues can maintain, does.
4. **Join a community, and just do the thing.** Follow the instructions exactly. Don't skip steps. Type it yourself instead of copy-pasting.
5. **Learn in public.** Post your progress, even small things. My LinkedIn posts helped me get two job offers in one day.
6. **Use certifications for the foundation, not the badge.** A pile of projects can still leave gaps. A good certification fills them.
7. **Find a mentor as early as you can.** It doesn't matter how experienced you are. Everything keeps changing.
8. **Fix your communication.** I hid behind emails for years because of my English. Don't wait 20 years like I did.
9. **Respect the foundation.** Beautiful charts on bad data will mislead people. Data quality and metadata are not extras.
10. **It's never too late.** I was over 40, my company had just closed, and I was back to being a student. A year later, I had a new career in a new industry.
