# Learn my language if you want to talk to me

## AI is coming

> "Healthcare and analytics are both constantly evolving."
> — Joshua Matlock, senior healthcare data analyst

After a year as a healthcare data specialist, I see this every day. The way we work with data now is totally different from the end of last year: our reporting has changed, our analysis is deeper, operational teams keep asking for more advanced insights, and different teams, including IT, work together much more closely.

I believe that within a year, using AI in data analysis will be a normal daily requirement in healthcare. I want to be ready.

So I enrolled in two programs at the **Johns Hopkins Whiting School of Engineering**, 19 weeks in total:
- **AI and Agentic AI in Healthcare Certificate**, for management and leadership,
- **Applications of AI and Agentic AI in Healthcare**, for practitioners and researchers.

A good friend, Joko P. Sutrisno, who has been growing his own journey in data science, shared these programs with me, encouraged me to join, and is now my personal mentor as I practice.

## Back to school

As part of my journey to understand the healthcare business, I also enrolled in the **Master of Applied Science (MAS) in Patient Safety & Healthcare Quality** at the **Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health.**

To be honest, I was very stressed writing my first proposal. Coming from a non-healthcare background, writing an academic paper about healthcare felt like a huge challenge.

I chose diagnostic errors as my topic, because Dr. David Newman-Toker explained in his lecture that they're among the most common and most dangerous errors in healthcare. In my paper, I proposed using AI to help prevent diagnostic errors in acute stroke cases. The idea of combining AI with real healthcare problems came from Dr. Daniel Byrne, a lecturer in the AI in Healthcare program and an amazing teacher and mentor.

Then I got this feedback from my teaching assistant:

> "This is an ambitious and thoughtfully developed diagnostic-safety topic that demonstrates a strong systems perspective and incorporates both decision support and closed-loop feedback."

Alhamdulillah! Through this process, I also learned how to reference scientific work properly and avoid plagiarism.

*(Bonus: in another course, I assessed the walkability of a pedestrian walkway in Madinah, right near my home. It was a fun way to see how a city's design affects community health.)*

## EFQM 2025 got data right

While choosing a quality framework for one of my courses, I discovered the **EFQM Model 2025**, a framework for organizational excellence.

Its criterion 5.4 asks organizations to apply **data governance** and ensure **data quality and reliability**, alongside advanced analytics and AI. Data management stops being a side project and becomes part of how excellence is measured. A dream come true!

Analytics is the part people see. But it depends on everything underneath. Without data management, analytics can mislead.

A simple healthcare example: a nurse sees a measurement on the screen. The database stores it differently. The report calculates it a third way. Another team reads it a fourth way. Each number looks correct, but the report no longer shows what really happened to the patient, and nobody owns the definition.

In my view, this foundation can grow in stages:
1. **Metadata and data quality.** Agree on definitions and write them down. Don't wait for an official structure to start. Make sure every number you report has a clear definition and a quality check.
2. **Data modeling and master data management**, so the system can grow without the definitions falling apart.
3. **Data governance:** formal ownership and named approvers.

*(This is just my view, based on what can start immediately. I haven't been part of an end-to-end data management program yet.)*

**Analytics shows you the answer. Data management tells you whether you can trust it.**

## Key takeaway

If you work in data or software, whether junior, experienced or senior: always connect with the business side. Step away from your desk. Ask people about their daily problems. Learn how they work, and offer real solutions.

As Dr. Albert Wu said in one of his patient safety lectures:

> "Automation and forcing functions are perhaps the strongest type of intervention."

*(For us, automation can mean automated reporting, data alerts, or AI-based decision support.)*

To build something meaningful for people, you first have to **learn their language.**
